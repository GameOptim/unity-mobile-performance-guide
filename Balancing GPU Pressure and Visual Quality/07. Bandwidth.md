# 4.7 Bandwidth

The previous sections mainly focused on **GPU Clocks** and related parameters affecting it.

However, GOT Online GPU Mode also collects another critical metric: **GPU Bandwidth**, which deserves close attention.

As we have seen, **GPU Clocks mainly affects frame rate**. High GPU Clocks force the GPU to run at high frequency, which also increases power consumption and heat.

But **GPU Bandwidth** is a separate dimension of metric that **primarily impacts power consumption and thermal issues**, independent of GPU Clocks.

![92](https://uwa-ducument-img.oss-cn-beijing.aliyuncs.com/GameOptim/4.7Bandwidth/92.png)

In fact, **GPU pressure is a major cause of overheating** in many projects.

Evidence comes from hardware temperature data collected by GameOptim, including **CPU, GPU, and battery temperatures** on mobile devices.

Due to the compact size of mobile SoCs, components are physically close with poor heat dissipation.

When one chip overheats under high load, it raises the temperature of other chips as well.

This not only worsens the player’s hand feel but also triggers **thermal throttling**, further reducing frame rate.

Thus:

- If the **blue GPU temperature curve** is consistently high and dominates the temperature trend, **GPU pressure is the main cause of overheating**.
- Otherwise, the main cause is CPU pressure.

![93](https://uwa-ducument-img.oss-cn-beijing.aliyuncs.com/GameOptim/4.7Bandwidth/93.png)

Based on past project experience:

High main thread load, worker thread load, network traffic, or high I/O pressure mainly lead to **CPU overheating**.

Yet in many projects, **GPU temperature is even higher**.

In such cases, besides reducing GPU Clocks (as discussed earlier), **optimizing GPU Bandwidth is also extremely effective** for reducing heat and power consumption.

Previously, we concluded qualitatively (from experience and discussions with chip vendor engineers) that **GPU Bandwidth directly determines power consumption and heat**.

In fact, a **quantitative relationship** exists:

According to official Mali documentation:

**~1 GB/s bandwidth ≈ 80–100 mW power consumption**.

To put this in perspective:

- Idle or light apps: ~**1000–2000 mW** total power (healthy, low power state)
- High-performance games: often **4000–5000 mW**, sometimes even higher

Under heavy rendering and high frame rates, a game can easily reach **10 GB/s bandwidth**, causing **1000 mW power draw** — **~20% of total power**.

This is a huge contribution to heat and power drain, even before accounting for OS overhead, CPU, memory, and other components.

According to GameOptim’s large-scale testing:

- Low-end devices / casual games: bandwidth usually **2–3 GB/s**
- High-end devices / high frame rate + high quality: generally **≤ 5–6 GB/s**

Bandwidth varies significantly across manufacturers and models, so fixed values cannot be given.

However, **bandwidth analysis is highly effective** when a project suffers from severe battery drain or overheating.

------

To analyze bandwidth, we can break it down:

- **Read bandwidth** accounts for the majority of total bandwidth
- **Texture read bandwidth** is usually much larger than **vertex read bandwidth**

**Vertex read bandwidth** is determined by mesh data volume.

Optimizations for vertex pressure (covered earlier) also reduce vertex bandwidth, so dedicated optimization is often unnecessary.

**Texture read bandwidth** mainly comes from:

- Texture sampling
- RenderTexture usage

Many RenderTexture operations also generate high **write bandwidth**.

Common optimization methods:

## (1) Number of Sampled Textures

Excessive simultaneous texture samplings significantly increase bandwidth.

Since texture count is closely tied to visual quality, large reductions are difficult.

However, developers should check for **unnecessary or redundant samplings**.

## (2) Texture Compression Format

As mentioned in the memory chapter,

**proper texture compression reduces data per pixel**, directly lowering texture read bandwidth.

## (3) Texture Mipmap

As explained in detail in the memory chapter:

Enabling Mipmap for appropriate textures **greatly reduces bandwidth** via lower-resolution sampling at distance.

Details are not repeated here.

## (4) Proper Texture Filtering

Beyond using non-zero Mipmap levels, pay attention to:

- **Anisotropic filtering**
- **Trilinear filtering**

These improve visual quality but **increase sample points per pixel**, raising **Cache Miss** rate and bandwidth.

Recommendations:

- In Quality Settings: set **Anisotropic Textures = Per Texture**, NOT Forced On.
- Enable anisotropic filtering only for necessary textures, at low levels, or disable entirely.
- **Trilinear filtering samples 8 taps**, twice that of bilinear filtering. Use only when visually necessary.

GameOptim’s local resource detection can list all textures with anisotropic or trilinear filtering enabled.

![94](https://uwa-ducument-img.oss-cn-beijing.aliyuncs.com/GameOptim/4.7Bandwidth/94.png)

## (5) Reduce Render Resolution

Lowering render resolution reduces pixels and texture samples,

**directly and drastically reducing bandwidth**.

## (6) Reduce Copy Operations and Post‑Processing Sampling

Render Texture Copy operations (e.g., **Copy Color, Copy Depth** in URP) have high bandwidth cost.

Eliminate unnecessary copies.

In addition:

AA, Bloom, DOF, Blur, and other post effects perform **multiple high-resolution samplings**, causing heavy bandwidth usage.

These should be **tiered or disabled on low-end devices**.
